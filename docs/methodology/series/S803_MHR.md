## 1. What the series is
S803 is the chapter's **most-discussed dataset**: a single series consolidating Shaikh's Figures 8.3 and 8.4, both from **Bain (1951)**. Fig 8.3 is the scatter of 42 US-manufacturing industry-average **rates of profit on equity (ROE, 1936–40, after income taxes)** against **CR8 (share of value product supplied by the top 8 firms, 1935 Census)**; Fig 8.4 re-bins the same 42 industries into CR8 deciles, using **Demsetz's (1973b) corrections** to Bain's Table II. Three subseries: `S803-FIG83` (42-industry scatter, from `Appendix8_Bain42IndustryProfit.xlsx`), `S803-FIG84-BAIN` (Bain original deciles), `S803-FIG84-DEMSETZ` (Demsetz-corrected deciles, from `Appendix8_Bain42IndustryAggregates.xlsx` + `Appendix8_CorrectedBainData.xlsx`); 104 chopped rows. `content_type: cross_sectional` — the 42 observations are one 1936-40 cross-section; Fig 8.4 is that cross-section re-binned, not a second time point. Book framing (page-cited, p. 373): *"He carefully constructed a sample of profit rates on equity (ROE) and eight-firm concentration ratios (CR8) for forty-two industries comprising 335 firms over 1936–1940."*

## 3. Why this source (Shaikh's perspective) + rejected alternatives
Bain (1951) is **the foundational study of the structure-conduct-performance tradition** — the origin of the claim that concentrated industries earn persistently higher profit rates through monopoly power. Shaikh attacks it at the root, and does so with maximal force by **re-running Bain's regressions on Bain's own data** and showing the hypothesis fails on its author's terms: a simple linear fit gives β = 0.0521, **R² = 0.0781**; a quadratic gives R² = 0.1896 with a *non-monotonic* critical CR8\* = 49.24 (the wrong shape for a monopoly story); and the Demsetz-corrected decile grouping gives **R² = 0.033**. He then quotes Bain against himself: the *"fit … is obviously so poor that the inference of a rectilinear or other simple relationship of concentration to profits is not warranted."* Shaikh also exposes Bain's hidden premise — because Bain only had ROE (profit relative to net worth), he *"implicitly assumed that different industries have the same ratio of liabilities to assets,"* i.e. identical leverage — which motivates the pivot to the theoretically correct profit-*on-assets* measure in Stigler (S804). Citing the SCP school's own patriarch, and letting his data and his admission do the refuting, is the sharpest possible move against the oligopoly-pricing school: real competition predicts exactly this weak, unstable, leverage-contaminated non-relationship.
**Rejected alternatives** (from `S803_research.json` extension_candidates): a modern 42-industry / CR8 / ROE reconstruction from **IRS SOI Corporation Source Book** profits + **Census Concentration Ratios** is blocked by (a) 1935-Census-code vs. modern-NAICS non-conformability and (b) the need to replicate Bain's sample-exclusion rules (<3 firms / limited data). Cross-sectional ⇒ `extension_status: not_applicable_cross_sectional`; any modern snapshot is a new series, not an extension (EPR).

## 4. Methodological-change exposure (concordance / classification)
S803 carries the chapter's **richest concordance touch**: it embeds both **1935 Census industry (SIC-precursor) numbers** *and* **CR8 concentration ratios** across 42 industries (plus the Demsetz 1973b regrouping). `CH08_review.json` flags `touches_concordance` manifest=true / canonical registry=None (review M3) — sync-only; the handling is correct: **no SIC→NAICS crosswalk is applied**, because RSCD reproduces the historical Bain/Demsetz tables verbatim. The RSCD Census SIC↔NAICS chain (`concordances/_sources/naics/`) begins at 1987 SIC and cannot reach Bain's 1935 categories — an **irrecoverable classification wall below the bottom of the bridge** (cf. `IO_CHANGE_TIMELINE.md`: last SIC benchmark 1992, first NAICS 1997, pre-1997 tables "should not be used as a time series"). **No NIPA/BEA-benchmark exposure**: these are firm-accounting ratios, not BEA magnitudes.
